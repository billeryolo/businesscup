# Turn one-minute checks into trusted answers

ReadBuddy can give parents a credible, "diagnosis-like" picture, but only as a **risk-and-profile screen, never a diagnosis**. The research behind it is strong. Oral reading fluency (words correct per minute, WCPM) is the most-validated quick indicator of reading in grades 1–4. In kindergarten, number identification and "which number is bigger" tasks predict later math at r ≈ .40–.79. Published free benchmarks exist for both: Hasbrouck & Tindal for reading and Acadience Math for numbers. The limits are just as clear. A single one-minute read is noisy: even human-scored WCPM had test-retest reliability of only about .69, and real weekly growth is just 1–2 words. Browser speech recognition overestimates child WCPM by roughly 5–10%. Two things turn a score into an analysis a parent can act on: **why** the child struggles, which takes a few extra subskill tasks, and **a plan matched to that cause**, which works best when the parent teaches a specific skill (d ≈ 0.65) rather than just reading together (small effects). The market gap is real. Most parents believe their child is on grade level (about 88%), while 40% of US 4th graders read below NAEP Basic. No consumer app hands parents a norm-referenced, teacher-ready report. The highest-value next steps are therefore an honest score with an uncertainty band, a "why" profile, a teacher-ready report, and growth tracked against the norm path. More games add little.

## A one-minute read is a reliable thermometer but a noisy single snapshot

The core of ReadBuddy rests on solid ground. A meta-analysis of oral reading CBM against standardized reading tests in grades 1–6 found a **"significant, strong overall correlation"** ([Reschly et al. 2009](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Curriculum-Based-Measurement-Oral-Reading-as-an/9984003959702771)). Oral reading also correlated more strongly with state comprehension tests than silent maze tasks did ([Shin 2017](https://conservancy.umn.edu/bitstream/handle/11299/191444/Shin_umn_0130E_18661.pdf?sequence=1)). Letter naming fluency has significant predictive validity for kindergarten reading ([U. Oregon](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu//bitstreams/69b56fe8-40c5-43d3-b031-8de7ba5d2600/download)). The **national 50th-percentile WCPM path** is public: grade 1 is 29 in winter and 60 in spring, grade 2 is 50, 84 and 100 across fall, winter and spring, and grade 3 is 83, 97 and 112 ([Reading Rockets / Hasbrouck & Tindal 2017](https://www.readingrockets.org/article/fluency-norms-chart-2017-update)). Saying "your child read 42 words; the typical winter 2nd grader reads 84" is defensible and instantly meaningful.

Screening accuracy, though, is only "adequate." Screener cut scores are set to reach **sensitivity around .70–.80**. Only some cut-setting methods kept sensitivity at or above .70 when grade-2 fluency was used to predict the grade-3 state test ([Montclair](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/developing-local-oral-reading-fluency-cut-scores-for-predicting-h/)). easyCBM targets above .80 ([easyCBM TR1801](https://brtprojects.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/TechRpt_1801ClassificationAcc.easyCBMGrade2.pdf)). Kentucky accepts mCLASS DIBELS 8 at **AUC ≥ .70**, which counts only as "fair" accuracy ([KY DOE](https://education.ky.gov:443/curriculum/conpro/engla/Documents/mClass_DIBELS_8th_Edition.pdf)). Any single cut will miss some struggling children and alarm some parents needlessly. Three bands ("on track / keep an eye / worth talking to the teacher") are more honest than a pass/fail verdict.

Single-session noise is the bigger product problem. In a 2026 study of US K–3 children reading at home, **test-retest reliability of human-scored WCPM was .693**. It was .69–.78 for readings of at least 60 seconds but collapsed to **.25–.30 for readings under 60 seconds and for kindergartners** ([Leite et al. 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-main.53.pdf)). DIBELS fluency scores carry standard errors of roughly 4–18 words, and Shanahan warns that changes smaller than the error cannot be read as growth ([Shanahan](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/were-getting-odd-reading-results-from-our-progress-monitoring-tests)). Meanwhile, expected growth is tiny: **about 1.5 words per week in grades 1–2 and about 1.0 in grade 3**, with "ambitious" growth at 2.0 and 1.5 ([Christ et al. 2010, citing Fuchs et al. 1993](https://www.readnaturally.com/userfiles/ckfiles/files/christ-silberglitt-yeo-cormier.pdf)). Growth is also faster in fall than spring ([Christ et al. 2010](https://www.readnaturally.com/userfiles/ckfiles/files/christ-silberglitt-yeo-cormier.pdf)). The consequence for ReadBuddy's weekly re-checks is direct. A week-to-week change under about 10 words should read "about the same." Trend claims need roughly six checks over 6–10 weeks, fitted with a line or rolling median. Benchmark moments should use the median of several passages. Hasbrouck & Tindal's guidance, as summarized by publishers, uses the average of two unpracticed readings ([Read Naturally](https://files.readnaturally.com/about-us/blog/interpreting-winter-assessment-data-and-setting-goals-for-spring)).

Speech recognition adds a known bias that can be corrected. Off-the-shelf Whisper **overestimated story-level WCPM by about 6–10%**, and the bias grew from +5.6% in kindergarten to +11.1% in grade 3. The cause is that larger models "auto-correct" children's misreadings and drop hesitations ([Leite et al. 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-main.53.pdf)). Hesitations and self-corrections, the most common error category at 10.9% of words, are precisely what ReadBuddy wants to report. Purpose-built, text-aware systems do far better. FLORA came within an average of **3.62 words** of human scores ([cited in Leite et al. 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-main.53.pdf)). Project CORE stayed within 0.15 SD on fixed-length passages but degraded on time-limited, variable-length reads (same source). Word-level error detection is weaker still: the best Dutch system reached only **MCC = .63** ([Molenaar et al. 2023](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/molenaar23_interspeech.pdf)). ReadBuddy should therefore align recognized speech against the known passage text rather than transcribing freely. It should require a full 60 seconds, calibrate out an upward bias, show a range rather than a single number, and let the parent replay and correct flagged words. Letter checks should use constrained single-item recognition, choosing among the 26 letters, which is far easier than recognizing open reading.

## Explaining *why* takes four more one-minute tasks, not finer error analysis

WCPM says *how* reading is going, not *why*. The International Dyslexia Association's screening guidance lists the components that separate causes. Kindergarten screening covers phonemic awareness, letter-sound association, rapid naming and phonological memory, and grade 1 adds word-reading fluency and oral vocabulary ([NJ IDA](https://nj.dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/18/2017/10/Universal-Screening-10-4-17.pdf)). Each adds a distinct parent-facing answer:

- **Letter-sound fluency** shows whether the child knows the code, not just letter names.
- **Nonsense-word fluency** isolates pure decoding from memorized sight words and context guessing, and it had the strongest validity support in a small kindergarten study ([letter fluency validity study](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c364t-2422beade187d33265bc6d29fcc56c99e823f767ad23b2693fb5f5e1de98b9a3)).
- **Rapid automatized naming** of letters or digits flags slow retrieval even when accuracy is fine. Symbol-based RAN predicts reading better than naming objects or colors ([Tennessee guide](https://www.tn.gov/content/dam/tn/education/special-education/rti/Screening_for_Characteristics_of_Dyslexia-A_Guide.pdf)). Kindergarten RAN correlates only **r = −.38** with later reading ([McWeeny et al.](https://learnlab.northwestern.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Proof_RRQ_Submission_McWeeny.pdf)), so it supplements other measures rather than standing alone. Acadience has now added RAN to its mainstream screener ([Acadience TR28](https://acadiencelearning.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/AcadienceRAN_IncrementalValidity_TechReport28.pdf)).
- **Phoneme segmenting** targets the core dyslexia weakness, but timed versions in fall of kindergarten classify poorly, and accuracy-based formats work better ([Zumeta, Vanderbilt](https://ir.vanderbilt.edu/items/6b8e63b8-bcaf-43e3-be30-c39cb6973d8e)). ReadBuddy should run it untimed and report it descriptively.
- **A short retell or comprehension question** after the read catches the fluent-but-not-understanding profile that WCPM misses.

Together these yield four plain-language profiles a parent can act on: *doesn't know the sounds yet*, *knows sounds but can't blend*, *accurate but slow*, and *reads well but understands little*.

Fine-grained miscue analysis is where ReadBuddy should hold back. Modified miscue profiles proved unstable, and researchers urged caution in inferring reading strategies from them ([Moore et al. 1986 record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........f2569b7e57a2e1769dbe733c3fd5d978)). Miscue patterns also shift with instruction and purpose ([critical review](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1080/10862967909547319)). No study found here validated error *types* for telling decoding, fluency and comprehension profiles apart. The defensible approach is to report **recurring, countable observations tied to an action**: "often said a word starting with the same letter → practice sounding out every letter," or "skipped small words → track with a finger." Each should carry a link to replay the audio and an "observed pattern, not a diagnosis" label. Self-corrections should not count as errors, which matches standard CBM practice.

## For numbers, test digits and magnitude, not counting aloud or speed drills

The math side has a stronger case for mattering than many parents realize. Across six longitudinal datasets, **school-entry math predicted later achievement with a coefficient of .33, versus .13 for reading and .07 for attention** ([Duncan et al. 2007](https://sites.uci.edu/gduncan/files/2021/03/School-readiness-pdf.pdf)). Kindergarten number competence predicted both level and growth through grade 3 ([Jordan et al. 2009](https://www.ovid.com/journals/depsy/pdf/10.1037/a0014939~early-math-matters-kindergarten-number-competence-and-later)).

The task choice matters. In grade 1, one-minute **number identification, quantity discrimination and missing-number** tasks predicted spring math at r = .72–.79. Kindergarten versions predicted at .46–.72. A "counting knowledge" measure managed only .35–.36, and the reviewers concluded that "strategic counting and magnitude comparison" are the key variables ([Gersten, Clarke & Jordan 2007](https://dwwlibrary.wested.org/files/5293918.pdf)). Comparing written numerals (r = .30) predicts math better than comparing dot clouds (r = .24) ([Schneider et al. 2017](https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/PAE/Team/Schneider/SchneiderEtAl2017.pdf)). Strikingly, a one-minute addition/subtraction probe at the start of grade 1 was **not** a significant predictor of later math difficulty ([Fuchs et al. 2006, in Gersten et al. 2007](https://dwwlibrary.wested.org/files/5293918.pdf)). ReadBuddy's math check for ages 5–7 should therefore lead with "name this number," "which is bigger," and "what comes next/missing." Fact speed belongs to ages 7–10, and oral counting is a weak discriminator.

Acadience Math publishes free criterion-referenced cut points from 542,407 students. For example, kindergarten Number Identification Fluency has benchmarks of 6, 14 and 25 at the beginning, middle and end of year. Children at or above benchmark have **about 80–90% odds** of meeting later goals, and those well below have about 10–20% ([Acadience Math Benchmark Goals](https://acadiencelearning.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/AcadienceMath_Benchmark_Goals.pdf)). These can serve as reference points but must not be labelled "Acadience scores." The norms assume trained, standardized administration, and aimswebPlus states that modifications invalidate its norms ([aimswebPlus glossary](https://www.marshfieldschools.org/cms/lib/WI01919828/Centricity/Domain/82/AIMS%20Plus%20Glossary.pdf)). Repeated testing has its own limits. Quantity discrimination and next-number tasks showed low delayed alternate-form reliability under weekly testing ([EJ1090835](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1090835.pdf)), and only number identification grew linearly over months ([UMN](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/establishing-technically-adequate-measures-of-progress-in-early-n/)). Benchmark-implied growth is only about 0.2–0.5 items per week, our calculation from the Acadience goals. A 2024 review also found no early-numeracy measure with content-validity evidence strong enough to recommend for clinical use ([COSMIN review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11412641/)). That is one more reason to describe results rather than diagnose.

Fact response times offer a genuinely insightful "why." Children with math learning difficulties usually catch up on counting, but **fact-retrieval deficits persist** ([PMC3779611](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3779611)). Children who count show response times that rise with problem size ([review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.01208)). ReadBuddy can log per-item latency and tell parents "likely counting on these facts, likely remembered on those," judged relative to the child's own fastest items. No validated app-level cutoff exists, so this should stay an observation. Base rates call for humility in any math risk flag. Dyscalculia affects about 5–7% of children ([FUNA-DB](https://tuhat.helsinki.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/529344064/Author_final_version_Psychometrics_FUNA-DB.pdf)). At a 6% base rate, even a screener with 85% sensitivity and 85% specificity would be right only about **27% of the time it flagged a child** (our calculation). Achievement-based screens "have sensitivity but lack specificity" ([review via utupub](https://www.utupub.fi/bitstreams/19eff8a2-3838-4de9-ab19-74f5ce98f823/download)).

## The 4-week plan works when the parent teaches one targeted skill

The type of parent involvement decides the effect. In Sénéchal's K–3 synthesis, family literacy interventions averaged **d = 0.65–0.68**, roughly 10 points on a standard test. Parents teaching specific literacy skills was about twice as effective as parents listening to children read ([Sénéchal 2006 report](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090102233937/http://purl.access.gpo.gov/GPO/LPS75564); [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/flang.2025.1540562/full)). Broad family-literacy programs showed only a "significant, but small" effect ([Van Steensel et al. 2011](https://lofflinannotations.blogspot.com/2011/03/how-effective-are-family-literacy.html)). Home interventions for ages 3–5 averaged just **d = 0.10** ([Cahoon et al. 2023](https://pure.ulster.ac.uk/en/publications/meta-analyses-and-narrative-review-of-home-based-interventions-to/)). Dialogic reading lifts expressive vocabulary (d = 0.59) but much less for at-risk children (d = 0.13 vs 0.53) ([Mol et al. 2008](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2899524/view)). The AI story generator is therefore a good vocabulary and oral-language tool for ages 4–5, as long as the parent asks open "wh" questions. It is not a fix for a decoding problem.

The weakness-to-activity map is well supported:

- **Accurate but slow readers:** repeated reading (fluency ES .75, comprehension .73), using echo or paired reading first ([Therrien 2004 via ASHA](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/903ec865-92fd-4ca5-9cea-8534b161420a)).
- **Inaccurate decoders and first-letter guessers:** explicit letter-sound and blending routines, with error prompts at the letter level ("look at the middle letters") rather than "what makes sense." Parent Direct Instruction and Paired Reading beat simply hearing the child read ([Leach & Siddall](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0144341950150209)).
- **Ages 4–6 with weak number sense:** a linear 1–10 number-board game. Four 15-minute sessions eliminated SES gaps in number estimation, in a trial WWC rated "meets standards without reservations" ([Siegler & Ramani](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/cs/research-teaching/docs/SieglerBoardGamesCDPerp2009.pdf); [WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/73725)).
- **Ages 6–10 with slow facts:** model the fact, give immediate correction (cover-copy-compare), and set a personal-best goal. Practice without modeling had negligible effects ([Codding et al. 2011](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/meta-analysis-of-mathematic-basic-fact-fluency-interventions-a-component-analysis/811676593792483330-5194)).

Apps help most when an adult is present. Across 36 studies, educational apps averaged **+0.31 SD**, with larger effects for preschool and constrained skills like letters and counting, and smaller ones on standardized tests and comprehension ([Kim et al. 2021](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1323865.pdf)). GraphoGame's overall effect on word reading was **g = −0.02, but 0.48 with high adult interaction** ([McTigue et al. via Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2020.00132)). In caregiver math programs, ongoing coaching beat one-off workshops (g = 0.26 overall) ([Nelson et al.](https://ecrhub.org/publications/meta-analysis-and-quality-review-mathematics-interventions-conducted-informal-learning); [Li et al. 2026](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/sage/unlocking-early-mathematics-potential-at-home-a-meta-analysis-of-the-8fwdzLnVD5)). Personalization is the evidence-backed nudge. READY4K's small weekly texts raised parent involvement by 0.15–0.29 SD and child literacy by about 0.11 SD ([York, Loeb & Doss](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20659)). Level-matched texts made kindergartners **63% more likely** to read at a higher level than generic texts ([Doss et al. 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24450)). Graphed progress with decision rules has a classic effect of about 0.70 ([Fuchs & Fuchs 1986](https://arch.neicon.ru/xmlui/handle/123456789/3040273)). Broader formative-assessment estimates are closer to 0.20 ([Kingston & Nash summary](https://www.gettingsmart.com/2018/07/09/the-research-base-for-formative-assessment/)). A 4-week, 10–15-minute plan can honestly promise movement on one constrained skill, such as letter sounds, a fact family or fluency on practiced text. It cannot promise a jump in reading level. Because the app's own re-check will overstate gains, re-checks should use new passages.

## Parents lack an objective benchmark, and that is the paid value

The emotional hook is a perception gap. In Gallup/Learning Heroes polling, **88% of parents say their child reads on grade level** ([PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/study-shows-parents-overestimate-their-students-academic-progress)). Yet **40% of 4th graders score below NAEP Basic**, the highest share since 2002 ([Nation's Report Card](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reports/reading/2024/g4_8/national-trends)). Only about 30% of parents feel extremely confident they know grade-level expectations ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/?p=1039511)). Parents state a willingness to pay of about **$357/month for tutoring**, but cost is the top barrier ([EdChoice/Morning Consult](https://edchoice.morningconsultintelligence.com/assets/329770.pdf); [50CAN](https://50can.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/50CAN_EducationalOpportunitySurvey_2ndEdition.pdf)).

Competitors leave the interpretation layer open. Ello offers progress reports and a marketing claim with no published method ([ello.com](https://www.ello.com)), and its App Store pricing runs $14.99 per month or $139 per year ([AppPricingLab](https://apppricinglab.com/app/apple/1536720182)). The evidence-rated products, Lexia Core5 "Strong" and Amira ESSA Level II, sell to schools ([Lexia](https://www.lexialearning.com/research/essa-evidence); [WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/95242)). Microsoft Reading Coach is free for home use ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/01/18/microsoft-makes-its-ai-powered-reading-tutor-free)). Read-aloud practice is therefore commoditized, while the norm-referenced verdict, the "why," the plan and the teacher-ready artifact are not. One caveat: we did not verify hands-on that no consumer app shows WCPM percentiles. Retention follows the same logic. Insufficient usage (37%) and cost (35%) top subscription cancellation reasons ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-churn-reasons-how-to-fix.md)), so visible growth matters more than more content.

The legal boundary is clear. NCIL defines screening as estimating risk, not determining dyslexia ([NCIL 2026](https://improvingliteracy.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/ncil-screening-for-dyslexia.pdf)). State guides speak of screening for "characteristics of dyslexia" ([Tennessee](https://www.tn.gov/content/dam/tn/education/special-education/rti/Screening_for_Characteristics_of_Dyslexia-A_Guide.pdf)). About 40 or more states already require K–2 or K–3 screening ([Chalkbeat](https://www.chalkbeat.org/colorado/2025/04/01/dyslexia-screening-bill-clears-key-legislative-committee)). Pointing parents to the school's screening is therefore both accurate and protective. Software that diagnoses a condition moves toward FDA medical-device territory ([FDA presentation](https://www.fda.gov/media/189834/download)). The FTC has barred unsubstantiated claims about academic benefits ([Jungle Rangers case](https://www.theregister.com/2015/01/22/ftc_raps_developer_for_lame_game_braintrain_claim/)) and fined ABCmouse **$10M** over hard-to-cancel billing ([EdWeek Market Brief](https://marketbrief.edweek.org/marketplace-k-12/ftc-reaches-10-million-settlement-age-learning-inc/)). The amended COPPA Rule, in force since April 22, 2026, treats **voiceprints as personal information**. It allows consent-free audio only when the audio is used solely to respond and then deleted immediately ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-final-coppa-rule-amendments)). It also requires operators to oversee third-party processors such as AI APIs ([Promise Legal](https://blog.promise.legal/coppa-2025-amendments-edtech-compliance/)). Two risks for ReadBuddy follow from this, both unverified for the team's exact setup. Browser speech recognition may send audio to the browser vendor's servers, which would undercut a "voice stays on the phone" claim. And sending transcripts or child details to DeepSeek needs consent gating and a no-retention arrangement.

### Wording guardrails

| Say | Don't say |
|---|---|
| "Reading fluency check," "compared with published national reference levels (Hasbrouck & Tindal 2017)" | "Diagnoses dyslexia/dyscalculia," "detects learning disabilities," "ADHD" |
| "On track / Keep an eye / Worth talking to the teacher" | "Failed," "at risk of dyslexia" as a label, or a raw percentile shown as exact |
| "Signs worth checking; not a diagnosis. If low across several checks, share this with your child's teacher." | "Your child has…," "proven to raise reading levels," "grade level in 4 weeks" |
| "Observed pattern: often guessed from the first letter" | "Your child uses visual cues instead of meaning" (unvalidated miscue typing) |
| "Likely counting / likely remembered" (math facts) | "Acadience score," "DIBELS score," "norm-referenced percentile" for tasks we did not norm |

## Prioritized product menu

Effort assumes the current PWA, which already has checks, verdicts, a plan, games, AI stories and weekly re-checks: S is about 1–3 days, M about 1–2 weeks, L about 3 or more weeks. The top tier strengthens credibility and is cheap. The second tier adds the "why." The third is about differentiation and growth.

## Conclusion

The most important shift is that ReadBuddy's value lies in **interpretation**, not measurement. The one-minute read is already well validated, and free tools already listen to children read. What no consumer product does is say, credibly and kindly: here is where your child stands against a national reference, here is the likely reason, here is what to do tonight, and here is a page to hand the teacher. The evidence also points to a counterintuitive design rule. The more honest the app is about noise, through ranges, "about the same" and waiting for trends, the more trustworthy its verdicts become. The app's weakest technical link, browser speech recognition on young children, is exactly where false precision would do the most damage.

The second takeaway is that the "diagnosis-like" picture comes from **profiles across several short tasks over several weeks**, not from any single score. Persistence over time is also what separates a responsible risk flag from a false alarm, and it is what schools and clinicians will want to see. The fastest credible path is three steps: Tier 1 items 1–6, then the reading "why" profile (#7) and the teacher report (#12). Together these turn existing features into the "assessment and plan a tutor would give you." Open evidence questions remain: exact pooled accuracy figures (Kilgus 2014), validated word-level ASR for struggling US readers, and any validation of app-administered checks against standardized ones. ReadBuddy's own data, once parents correct scores in the app, could eventually begin to answer them.
